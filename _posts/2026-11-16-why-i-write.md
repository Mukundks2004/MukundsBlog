In my Year 12 Advanced English class we studied George Orwell's text, [*Why I Write*](https://www.orwellfoundation.com/the-orwell-foundation/orwell/essays-and-other-works/why-i-write/). In it, Orwell proposes four great motives for writing that he argues exist in every writer. They are:

1. Sheer Egoism: writing from a "desire to seem clever, to be talked about, to be remembered after death, to get your own back on grown-ups who snubbed you in childhood, etc."
2. Aesthetic Enthusiasm: To create a piece of art having "pleasure in the impact of one sound on another, in the firmness of good prose or the rhythm of a good story."
3. Historical Impulse: a "desire to see things as they are, to find out true facts and store them up for the use of posterity."
4. Political Purpose: Orwell argues that "no book is genuinely free from political bias" and cites that all people have a "desire to push the world in a certain direction" even going so far as to say that "the opinion that art should have nothing to do with politics is itself a political attitude."

I'm disappointed that I didn't understand these points on my first reading. Coming back years later, I largely agree with Orwell! I've distilled his essay down to the following mnemonic.

The three motives are to:
1. Show (what you've made) - point #1
2. Save (an idea) - point #3
3. Send (a message) - point #4

Point #2 is covered for free by all three.

Some writers feel compelled to produce something for its own sake. A superficial desire, and nothing more. Others use writing as a therapeutic tool while still others wish to create a work that serves a purpose, like an informative pamphlet. The above motives are implicit and subconscious in the generative process. But when *I* write, I'm very aware of these forces and how they change the direction of the piece.

What's interesting is that once you start being mindful of the motives, you quickly realise that they are not orthogonal. To lean towards one motive often means to lean away from another.

This blog has many articles but it is one single work with one motive. When I started it in 2024, I intended to talk about one thing and one thing only: the IDeA cadetship. For example, how to choose a permanent team, how to make the most of the program at different stages, why your 'rotations partner' is so important, how to be an effective mentor, what subjects at uni deserve the most attention, etc. At the time the cadetship was the biggest part of my life and I spent a great deal of time reflecting on my experiences with different parts of it. Even writing about university as a standalone topic was not what I wanted. So the entire blog was motivated purely by 'Save'. To write down the things I would tell my friends about. These were refined, structured arguments that I had already communicated once or twice. They were not nebulous, yet-to-be-defined concepts. It would not be wrong to say that the purpose of the blog at this stage was to document.

I was very clear about it in my ['about' page](https://mukunds.blog/about/) where I wrote "I want to be allowed to finish the generative process that’s started when someone poses a question. I think it is good to tie these related questions together too, and make sure my thoughts are gathered and my answers are consistent."

You can also see this philosophy in the first few articles I drafted. For example, my [love letter to the witcher books](https://mukunds.blog/posts/im-obsessed-with-the-witcher-books/). It was long-winded (6000 words, which is long for a blog) since I never left an opinion out and I was reluctant to remove anything once it was written down. Even if one sentence directly took away from another point, I was more likely to adapt my claim and present both views rather than cut something from the final post. My train of thought is visible as I wrote organically, I didn't organise the post prior to writing it. In some places I would diverge from what felt like the broader point to note down what I was thinking at the time. Brevity and large-scale efficiency were just not things I valued.

Then I wrote my first technical article, on [Variance in C#](https://mukunds.blog/posts/what-is-the-co-in-covariant/). 'Save' began to take a back seat. This article had a new idea in it: interpreting C# typed generic variance as the mathematical, categorical, functoral variance. Because it was *my* new idea, I had a lot of control over how to communicate my point. So I kept trying to lean into the maths side of it, trying to invest more in 'Show'ing for the sake of my ego. There wasn't a need to 'Save' everything because the value in the post came from the single new claim in the article and there wasn't much room for opinion or commentary here.

But by choosing 'Show', by being biased in favour of using more technical language, by leaving ambiguity and implying there was more complexity than there was, I had to sacrifice 'seeing things as they are'. I was no longer writing all my ideas down in clear terms.

The exact nature of the 'Show' era shifted over time. At one point I reverted to the lengthy style but this time with emphasis on purple prose. Later I would try to use as many esoteric idioms and phrases as I could. But the essence of sheer egoism remained. That is, until recently, when I discovered a blog called ["It Is Trish"](https://itistrish.com/). I came across it when looking for inspiration for a new blog name, and it really changed my perspective on blogging.

There are lots of problems with how I blog. Firstly, it is difficult to be inspired because I need to have either a new idea or an experience as a subject, and these aren't common. Then, when I find a subject, if it is too big or important I usually delay writing about it. I let the subject stew in my mind so I'm able to explore my feelings about it. Then comes the writing process which is painful and time consuming. It shouldn't have to be either of these. And editing takes a shocking amount of time, usually double the writing time. My reward for this? A lengthy article that is extremely hard to read and very boring. It isn't even fair to call a collection of these sorts of articles a blog, because some content is technical, some content is fiction, and some content is just me rambling. A better word would be portfolio, or anthology.

Trish's blog is everything I want mine to be. Her posts have so much personality. She doesn't seem to go for "great big ideas" but she more than makes up for it in character, relatability, and friendliness. Her posts are simple and sometimes even unoriginal but are so easy to follow. They are very human. For example, *[Simple Days Are the Best Days](https://itistrish.com/2025/08/22/simple-days-are-the-best-days/)*, or *[Ask the Invasive Questions](https://itistrish.com/2025/07/29/ask-the-invasive-questions/)*. I feel close to the author because of her cozy, informal style and natural way of thinking. And no matter what she writes about, there is always some original component/value she adds through her commentary. Her blog posts are hardly ever longer than 300 words, so she's been able to post regularly for upwards of 15 years. It is not sustainable for me to keep blogging as frequently as Trish does for as long as she has been with my style of writing.

So, I think it is time to usher in a new era of blogging powered by a new motive- the desire to prioritise communication of ideas ('Send') and to be as efficient as possible with the message. Which may mean no more long and unstructured articles. And may also mean that my posts take on a more humble tone. In exchange, you can expect more frequent posts and more identity in them. In a way this era has already begun. When I retroactively edit my posts now, I am not afraid to cut back on words. [This article on 'The Culture of Sydney'](https://mukunds.blog/posts/being-a-living-part-of-sydney/) was once a collection of distantly related thoughts with no direction, and now it is organised and tries to synthesise observations, statements, opinions, and deductions into something new. A thesis has been developed and whole paragraphs that take away from it have been pruned. It is hard to let go of my old values but it is something I know I want to do.

I hope that with this change my personal blog will finally begin reading like a personal blog.
