In year 12 Advanced English we studied George Orwell's text, *Why I Write*. In it, Orwell discusses the four great motives for writing that he argues exist in every writer. They  are (summary lifted from the Wikipedia):

- Sheer egoism: writing from a "desire to seem clever, to be talked about, to be remembered after death, to get your own back on grown-ups who snubbed you in childhood, etc."
- Aesthetic Enthusiasm: To create a piece of art having "pleasure in the impact of one sound on another, in the firmness of good prose or the rhythm of a good story."
- Historical Impulse: a "desire to see things as they are, to find out true facts and store them up for the use of posterity."
- Political purpose: Orwell argues that "no book is genuinely free from political bias" and cites that all people have a "desire to push the world in a certain direction" even going so far as to say that "the opinion that art should have nothing to do with politics is itself a political attitude."

I feel disappointed I didn't absorb the main message on my first reading, because I largely agree with Orwell's thesis and learning about it earlier would have noticeable changed my writing. I've distill the proposition down to the following mnemonic:

The three motives are to
1) Show (what you've made) - point #1
2) Save (an idea) - point #3
3) Send (a message) - point #4

Point 2 is covered for free by all three.

For some writers, they feel compelled to produce something for the sake of itself, like some kind of art. Or a result with some effect, like an informative pamphlet. The above motives are implicit and subconscious in the generative process. But when *I* write, I'm very aware of these forces and how they change the direction of the piece.

What's interesting is that once you become mindful of the motives you realise that they are not orthogonal. To lean towards one motive often means to lean away from another.

This blog is one single work with many parts. When I started it in 2024 I intended to talk about one thing and one thing only: the IDeA cadetship and all my thoughts on it. For example, how to choose a permanent team, how to make the most of the program at different stages, why your 'rotations partner' is so important, how to be an effective mentor, what subjects at uni deserve the most attention, etc. At the time the cadetship was the biggest part of my life and I spent a great deal of time reflecting on my experiences with different parts of it. Even writing about university as a standalone topic was not what I intended. So the entire blog was motivated by just one point, 'Save', to write down what I would tell my friends about. All the things I wanted to write about were refined, structured arguments that I had already communicated once or twice. They were not nebulous, future concepts. It would not be wrong to say that the purpose of the blog was to document.

You can see this in the first few articles I drafted. For example, my love letter to the witcher books (link). It was hugely long winded (x words) since I never left anything out and I was reluctant to remove anything I wrote unless it directly took away from another point. Even if it did, I was more likely to adapt my claim and present both views rather than cut a thought from the final post. You can even see my train of thought in my writing as I wrote organically (I didn't structure the post prior to writing it). In some places I would diverge from what felt like the broader point to capture what I was thinking at the time. Brevity and large-scale efficiency were just not things I valued.

Then I wrote my first technical article, on Variance in C# (link it). 'Save' began to take a back seat. This article had a new idea in it: the idea of interpreting C# typed generic variance as the mathematical, categorical, functoral variance. Because it was *my* new idea, I had a lot of control over how to communicate my point. So I kept trying to lean into the maths side of it, trying to invest more in 'Show'ing for the sake of my ego. There wasn't a need to 'Save' every idea because the value came from the single new claim in the article. But being biased in favour of using more technical language, and leaving ambiguity while implying there was more complexity than there often was, came at the direct cost of 'seeing things as they are' by writing all my ideas down in clear terms.

The exact nature of my 'Show' era shifted over time. At one point I reverted to the lengthy style but this time with emphasis on purple prose. At another point I would try to use as many esoteric idioms and phrases as I could. But the essence of sheer egoism remained until recently, when I discovered this blog by (it is tiffany). I came across it when looking for inspiration for a new blog name, and it really changed my view on blogging.

There are lots of problems with how I blog. Firstly it is difficult to come up with blog post ideas because I need to have either a new idea or experience as a subject and these aren't common. Then when I find a subject, if it is too big or important I usually delay writing about it so I can do it justice. Then comes the writing process which is painful and time consuming. It shouldn't be either. And editing takes a shocking amount of time, usually double the writing time. My reward for this? A lengthy, dry article that is extremely hard to read. It isn't even fair to call a collection of these sorts of articles a blog, because some content is technical and other content is just ramblings.

Tilly's blogs have so much personality. What she lacks in "great big ideas" she makes up for in relatability and friendliness. Her theses are simple and sometimes even unoriginal but are so easy to understand. They are very human. For example, X, or Y. Her blog posts are hardly ever longer than 200 words and so she's been able to post regularly (like monthly at least) for upwards of 15 years. It is not sustainable for me to keep blogging as frequently as tilly does for as long as she has with my style of writing.

So I think it is time to usher in a new era of blogging powered by a new motive- the desire to communicate ('Send') ideas cleanly and to be as efficient as possible with the message. Which may mean no more long and unstructured articles. And may also mean that the posts take on a more humble tone. In exchange you can expect more frequent posts and a bit more identity in them. I've already begun. When I edit my posts now, I am not afraid to cut back on words. This article (link living part of Sydney) was once purely a collection of partially related thoughts, and now it is organized and tries to synthesize many observations, statements, opinions, and deductions into a single new idea. Future posts will have more relaxed restrictions so I can write without the pressures of adding value or looking intelligent.

I hope that with this change my blog will finally begin reading like a blog.
