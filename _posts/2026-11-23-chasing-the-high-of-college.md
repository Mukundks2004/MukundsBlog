A lot of people are nostalgic for their college days. They often cite missing the crazy bacchanalia, day drinking, and no responsibilities. But is this really it?

The college environment is the perfect combination of everything a person could ever need to be satisfied and happy:

- Being young and inexperienced and having the energy/being given the freedom to try new things.
- As a young person, being surrounded by other young people constantly. Both new people and recurring faces. People are willing and able to connect platonically and romantically. And you tend to gravitate towards like-minded people too, so it is so easy to find 'your people', even as you grow and change.
- Convenience as everything you could want- companionship, necessities, third spaces, are all within walking distance. And the campuses are beautiful and well-maintained.
- Structure you can choose to respect, oriented around daily routines like sleep, meals, classes, and extracurriculars
- Predictable and controllable novelty in the form of new classes, new clubs, and new people
- The psychological freedom to experiment, to fail, and to be rejected
- Achievable goals that have been set for you, gradually increasing in difficulty and culminating in a 'final' goal: graduation


These aspects of life are more than just things to be grateful for. Together, they compromise a wholesome life!

When you graduate, one by one, all these elements fall away. The physical immediacy of your social circle is replaced by a lonely commute to work, instead of having a social calendar prepared for you, it is on you to create your own framework to make friends, instead of a clear finish line and unambiguous purpose, there are big open questions about life and meaning, and no-one is offering advice, suggesting you stay on your post-college path, or prompting you to do something different which can leave you lost. As Molly McAdam says, the "novelty flatlines into mundane routine". This might not hit you all at once. You might lose friends gradually and find yourself questioning your direction more and more with each passing day. When you finally reminisce years later, it isn't hard to trace each perceived deficit to a source.

There is a motivation behind examining these points in detail: essentially 100% of this is possible to reverse-engineer and recreate inorganically.

The exception being having your 'firsts'- but to be honest, that initial, overwhelming phase of college where those things do happen never lasts more than a few weeks anyway.

Surrounding yourself with other young people is just a matter of being the sort of person that attracts young people (because aside from a 100-person country town, there will always be other young people around).

Structure and novelty are both byproducts of having a lifestyle that you are in control of, being able to modulate how rigid or flexible you want to be.

Psychological freedom is purely psychological, that should speak for itself.

And achievable goals and progression, potentially the most vague and hard-to-define item on the list, doesn't have to be more complex than exploring the world, finding things you enjoy and are fulfilled by, and making them your priorities.

Most college students spend their years unthinkingly hiking up the college mountain (or sliding down it, if you have that kind of major). And that is a totally fine way to enjoy all of the above, if that is your end goal. But it certainly is not the only way, nor is it the best. Don't think of all of this as a 'college substitute'. Instead, I'd argue that the focused and deliberate approach to chasing the high is simply a more sustainable approach to living.
