An isomorphism is a morphism $$f : A \to B$$ if there exists an inverse $$g : B \to A$$ such that $$f \circ g = Id_B$$ and $$g \circ f = Id_A$$.

In GEB, Hofstadter popularises a version of the isomorphism defined the following way: *The word isomorphism applies when two complex structures can be mapped onto each other, in such a way that to each part of one structure there is a corresponding part in the other structure, where 'corresponding' means that the two parts play similar roles in their respective structures*. Given the age of the book, the relative recency of category theory in popular mathematics, and Hofstadter's motivation, I don't think it is fair to criticise his definition on the basis that it is not rigorous. In fact, I like that he has a characteristic way he uses it in the book- as an information preserving transformation, with emphasis on the information preserving part. He famously exemplifies an isomorphism between pieces of music and record players. And another between the **pq** system and natural addition. Whenever I've needed a word for a similar correspondence- not quite a unambiguous one to one mapping, but two systems with noticeable duality and identical information, I think of GEB and of 'Hofstadter Isomorphisms'.

I want to introduce a three way Hofstadter Isomorphism in music, and then make the connection between this correspondence and a similar psychological correspondence I identify with- an isomorphism of isomorphisms.

The story begins in the car when you turn the radio on and change the station to KIIS 106.5. It's Taylor's *New Romantics*- a song that means a lot to you, and one you can't resist singing along to. The song is upbeat but the melody isn't so straightforward. The chorus comes on just as you merge onto the M5 and hit the gas to accelerate.

*Cause baby I could build a castle! Out of all the bricks they threw at me-e-e...*

You empathise with Taylor, not because you've had bricks thrown at you or because you're moved by the lyrics, but because of an air about the line. It's unorthodox, it throws you off. It starts with a big claim- she could build a castle! She stretches that word to give you time to mull over it (?) and think why, and then it comes- because she's no stranger to resistance. It's a build-up followed by a reveal. And the reveal is not a moment of victory but one of vulnerability. She is sharing something many of us would be reluctant to admit, even if it is in a dramatic and unashamed way. Here, the feeling of insurmountable opposition is powered by the gradual descent in the fifth bar of the chorus- it is simulating falling. The sound of the music is the first of the three equivalent systems.

It took me a few listens and a bit of focus to identify 'gradual descent' as the source of the feeling. And full disclosure, I had to use a tool to do it: music notation. Even after my 22 years on earth listening to music, it blows my mind that there is a concrete way to translate between a feeling, in this case a descent, and an algebraic, symbolic representation. It goes "d d d b b a a g g f# e" (insert pic). You can SEE the cascade, which I think is the word that best describes this bar. Every note in the sheet music plays the same role as every toned word in the lyrics, hence the information preserving transformation. This hard, inspectable, rigorous mathematical representation of music is the second of the systems.

The third is a dimension you might not have expected- contemporary dance. You can't see me dancing in my room as the song plays right now, and my descriptive abilities are limited, so you'll have to imagine. But my brand of dance, even limited to a head and shoulders in the car, has to capture the rhythm of the song, the pacing of the lyrics, and loosely, the semantics. Properties of the song like the mood, or tone, or melody, are also able to be improvised upon. The dance has to 'fit' the song, it can't be too fast, or have movements that are untimed with the patterns of the lyrics. Both the music and the choreography have some common 'baseline' that they return to (for example, in the chorus) and from which variation occurs. And these repetitions and variations must be (loosely?) mirrored. In the full-bodied dance I'm picturing right now, the literal meaning of the word 'bricks' is not used. Instead, the cascade manifests as a physical descent as the knees bend simultaneously, further with each note, while the torso twists left and right to match the audible change in pitch on beat with the music. The hands are clenched, palms facing the chest, to represent the gravity of the disclosure. This is accompanied by a pained facial expression. Every part of the body, every bit of physical resource is utilised to translate the song, and its auditory effects, as best the dancer can.

Dance, music, and (word for western) notation comprise the first Tripartite isomorphism.

To me, each of these broad domains is representative of a portion of the Freudian theory of the spirit.

Music is Id, it is emotional, and raw, and uncontrollable. Music is too fast for you to think about it, at least on a first listen. By the time an effect has registered it has usually passed already. Maybe you sense a crescendo forming, and naively think you can anticipate the melody or dynamics- but at the last moment you are hit with a misdirect. It is impossible to reason with, compulsive, and primal.

Algebraic notation (?) is the ego. It is rational, and logical, and computable/calculable. You can reason with it by reading the notation, and re-reading it. You can edit the sheet music and observing the effect your editing. You can operate on it by shifting it up or down a semitone, via diminution or augmentation, and, of course, you can improvise upon it.

Dance is the superego. The superego is more complex than the Id or Ego, but one way to define it is as the judge that enforces ideals. In my world, dancing is built around ideals. Because while music is something I consume, and sheet music is something I can interact with minimally, dancing is something I produce. And in producing it I have to adhere to rules that constrain the dance in the dimensions of rhythm and speed and emotion and character.

Hofstadter argues that isomorphisms are important because humans internalise meaning through analogies. After I made this connection between these two parts of myself, I felt as though a truth had been uncovered. The same structure that relates how I perceive and interpret music applies to how I perceive and interpret the world.
