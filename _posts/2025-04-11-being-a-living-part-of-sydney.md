## What is Sydney?

A question posed so succinctly cannot have a definitive answer. The question demands to be explored in depth. It demands the answerer reference Australia's aboriginal heritage, colonial history, demographics and migration patterns, socio-cultural evolution, economic state, political partition, geographical boundaries, and countless other subjective ways of defining a city. I want to talk about how Sydney is its population. Not just the emergent property of a collection of people, but more precisely how insignificant individuals can have a very *felt* presence in a city of millions. And how a Sydneysider can control the size of their cultural contribution.

## People Make a Place

It is one thing to be a powerful politician, like our current prime minister, Albanese, or an influential public figure, like Danny Lim, or even people who made no conscious effort to stand out but ended up going down in history nonetheless, like Kathleen Folbigg. These people have made a measurable contribution to Sydney's recent history. But the very fact that they are named makes their contributions personal. As though the life they have lived and the things they have done are tied to their identities first and foremost and only through incidence are associated with Sydney itself. I think the most powerful contributions people make to the culture of a city is an *implicit one*. A contribution that can't be named, but one whose effects can be observed.

## How Can a Cultural Contribution be Implicit?

The Macca's at Town Hall on George St is the most famous Macca's in all of Sydney. The first time I dined at this dignified establishment was when I had graduated school and found myself spending late nights in the city. I grew up far away from fast food and I prefer stuff I've made myself, so there's really not a reason for me to seek out a fast food chain. But being there, on the grimy seats under the bright lights, was different to any other restaurant in the city. the Town Hall Macca's is the perfect blend of two things. Firstly security, I am always conscious of what I am going to eat and if a place has options I will like. If I see a chain I recognize, I know it will a) have standards (cleanliness, service, etc.), b) have options, and c) be reasonably cheap. Although Macca's is not renowned for having the healthiest foods, the fact that it is "safe" and familiar is enough for me to prefer eating fries there to taking a risk at a nicer place.

But the Town Hall Macca's boasts another quality that makes it appealing: it is busy. So busy, and there are so many people there, that *no one cares about you*. There's just too many orders and too much to do, too many drunk people throwing up, too many crackheads having sex in the bathrooms.

The first time I went to the THM I didn't even order, and no-one knew or cared. My friends and I sat at a table for literal hours. On top of all of this, the fact that the THM is essentially 24/7 is all I need. I could not wish for a better, more convenient third place. The THM is what it means to "enjoy Sydney like a local".

This is my experience with THM. Everyone has their own unique relationship with it, and you're not a real Sydneysider until you've been there. My mentor from work, for example, was telling me about what he got up to over the weekend and the THM was a central location in his recount. It means something to him too.

It's the fact that the THM is so small and approachable that allows people to come together to create and maintain what is really an icon of Sydney to people that actually live here. More than the Opera House or Harbour Bridge.

It is by being employed at this cultural institution that an insignificant, nameless teen can interact with so many people from all suburbs of Sydney and contribute to and truly be a part of Sydney's living heritage. To be one of the small team that has the honour of serving the community.

## The Central Tunnel

![Central tunnel](https://imgur.com/B9RJ1BB.png)

One other really fascinating example of "being a living part of Sydney" is busking in the tunnels. Yeah it is a bit shady and there are often people experiencing homelessness there. In fact, many buskers are just begging for money. But that tunnel (which I just learnt is called the Devonshire Street Tunnel) is universally known by all Sydneysiders. We've all walked there, probably run through there at some point as a student, and seen someone we knew there going the other way. Especially UTS students. I don't mean to idealise or romanticise homelessness, but singing to the public in that tunnel is to me the same as an overworked, underpaid teen doing the night shift at the THM. They're contributing to the shared experience of a place we've all been to and had not just exciting, but mundane experiences at.

Open up instagram and find a popular Sydney based shitposter. There are plenty, and all they do is make jokes about these shared experiences that are often tiny and forgettable to tourists- yet ones we all know about and relate to. Something I can talk about to anyone, assuming they will understand. And they always do.

## Creating Community at Scale

With the direction that last paragraph ended up taking, it may look like I'm trying to make the point that people cumulatively are what make the city. The same way a school is its students or a company is its employees. But, while this is true, that's not what I've primarily intended to express in this post- rather that an enormous shared culture hinges on a surprisingly small and *knowable* foundation. It is hard for individuals to impress their own value and individuality when part of a collective, especially as that collective scales.

Last year I attended the NCSS Summer Camp. How does a cohort of 100 kids develop a shared culture? Naturally some kids are more outgoing. Others by circumstance get picked on by the tutors or make fools of themselves in front of others, and an inside joke starts to develop. And I saw it firsthand, 5 - 10 kids started to become representative of that particular cohort of 100 students, because they were loud or fun or cool or smart or active on the Discord or just because. And most of them were invited back as returners. I saw this previously at WSC when I was 14 and again last month when I was in Adelaide for the ARC. The students that are invested in the comp, whose interviews make the video recap, who moderate the online community.

Maybe your class in high school had it too. The kids who would organize the birthday card for the teacher were often the ones that felt like they were part of the class and felt obligated to do something "for the class". And just like that they manifest their perceived centrality. They become the kid that organises.

By the time you get to the size of a local community- maybe 1000s of people, like the Ingleburn community- it becomes hard to have special individuals that represent everyone, whose voices are louder than the rest. People who identify with and are more involved with the spirit of the group than others. But they emerge, in the form of the guy who always goes way too big with Christmas decorations, or the librarian who has been around longer than most of us have been alive.

In a way I go to uni and build the UTS environment for non-uni students, but the scale of UTS (thousands attend UTS every day) makes me just that much less special than the small number with such considerable influence in other places. While employees of the Opera House and Sydney Bridge Climb and security guards at the ICC probably get a lot of traffic too, the exclusivity of these venues means that fewer people can relate to them. I mean, how many people do you know that you can guarantee have been to Sydney Aquarium without having asked them? But anyone can go to, and work at, the THM.

## In Conclusion

In conclusion I find it interesting that ordinary people, often unknowingly, build the shared experiences and culture of the city we live in.
